LINE ENCODING
Not all bits are sent as simple high/low voltage. Real cables use encoding schemes like NRZ, Manchester, or 4B5B to keep the signal clock-sync'd and reduce DC offset. But the principle is the same: voltage change = bit.
🔌
COPPER
Voltage changes in copper wire. Up to 10 Gbps. Cheap, common, EMI-sensitive.
💡
FIBRE
Light pulses in glass. 100+ Gbps. Immune to EMI. Long distance. Expensive.
📡
RADIO
EM waves through air. Wi-Fi, LTE, Bluetooth. Shared medium, interference possible.

LAYER 1 DEVICES — WHAT THEY DO
DEVICES HARDWARE
THESE DEVICES ONLY SEE BITS — NEVER HEADERS
🔁
REPEATER
Amplifies and retransmits the raw signal. Fights signal decay over long distances. Operates on bits only — no intelligence.
SIGNAL BOOST
🌐
HUB
A multi-port repeater. Receives a bit signal and blasts it to ALL ports. No filtering, no intelligence. Creates collision domains.
BROADCAST ALL
📡
MODEM
MOdulator-DEModulator. Converts digital bits to analog signals (and back) for transmission over telephone or cable lines.
DIGITAL ↔ ANALOG
💻
NIC (NETWORK INTERFACE CARD)
Lives in your computer. Converts the OS's digital data to physical signals and vice versa. Has a MAC address (that's L2) but handles L1 signalling.
L1 + L2 BRIDGE
🔌
CABLES & CONNECTORS
Cat5e / Cat6 (twisted pair), coaxial, fibre optic, RJ45 connectors. The physical medium itself IS the layer. Specs govern max length, bandwidth, shielding.
THE MEDIUM
⚠ L1 DEVICES CAN'T BLOCK TRAFFIC
Hubs and repeaters pass everything. A hub on your network = every device sees every packet. That's why switches (L2) replaced hubs.
KEY RULE:
L1 devices can't filter or read data.
They only amplify, repeat, or convert signals.

IEEE STANDARDS — THE RULES OF THE WIRE
STANDARDS IEEE
STANDARDS DEFINE WHAT "PHYSICAL" MEANS
🔌
IEEE 802.3
ETHERNET STANDARD
Defines everything about wired Ethernet: cable type, connector (RJ45), signal encoding, voltage levels, and max cable length.
10BASE-T
10 Mbps · Cat3 · 100m
100BASE-TX
100 Mbps · Cat5 · 100m
1000BASE-T
1 Gbps · Cat5e · 100m
10GBASE-T
10 Gbps · Cat6a · 100m
📶
IEEE 802.11
WI-FI STANDARD
Defines wireless Ethernet: radio frequency, channel width, modulation scheme (OFDM, DSSS), and transmission power limits.
802.11n
Wi-Fi 4 · 600 Mbps · 2.4/5 GHz
802.11ac
Wi-Fi 5 · 3.5 Gbps · 5 GHz
802.11ax
Wi-Fi 6 · 9.6 Gbps · 6 GHz
802.11be
Wi-Fi 7 · 46 Gbps · multi-band
WHY STANDARDS MATTER
Without IEEE standards, a Cisco NIC and an Intel NIC couldn't talk. Standards define the electrical contract — voltage, timing, modulation — so any two devices can physically exchange bits.
IEEE STANDARDS:

SECURITY NOTE
"If you can touch it, you own it." Physical access = game over. Layer 1 attacks: cutting cables, signal jamming (radio), tapping fibres, wiretapping copper. No software fix for physical access.
DUPLEX MODES:
Full-duplex = modern default.
Hubs = half-duplex. Switches = full-duplex.
