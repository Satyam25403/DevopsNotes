01
Your PC knows the destination IP (192.168.1.1) but needs the MAC to build an Ethernet frame. Layer 3 can't work without Layer 2.
02
ARP request is sent to FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF — the broadcast MAC. The switch floods it out every port. Every device on the LAN receives the frame.
03
Each device checks the target IP field. If it doesn't match their IP, they silently discard the frame. Only 192.168.1.1 keeps processing.

01
The router sees its IP in the request's target field. It sends a unicast ARP reply — directly to the requester's MAC. No broadcast needed for the reply.
02
The reply contains the router's MAC address. Your PC now has everything it needs: IP → MAC mapping. It can build the Ethernet frame and send the actual data.
03
Critical weakness: ARP is stateless and unauthenticated. Any device can send a fake ARP reply. If an attacker replies first, they intercept your traffic — ARP spoofing / MITM.
ARP REPLY
"192.168.1.1 is at DD:EE:FF:44:55:66"
The reply is unicast — only your PC receives it. Now the IP→MAC pair is cached in your ARP table so you don't broadcast again for a while.

After timeout, the entry is flushed. Next packet to that IP triggers a new ARP request. Static entries (like broadcast) never expire.
01
ARP table caches resolved IP→MAC pairs locally. Every OS keeps one. arp -a on Windows/Linux/macOS shows your current mappings.
02
Dynamic entries come from ARP responses and expire. Static entries are manually configured and never expire — used for permanent mappings like broadcast.
03
Caching prevents a broadcast for every single packet. Without it, a 1-second video stream to your router would generate ~30 ARP broadcasts per second.
04
ARP cache poisoning: attacker sends unsolicited ARP replies to overwrite your cache with their MAC. Now your traffic goes to them. No broadcast needed — OS accepts gratuitous ARPs.
